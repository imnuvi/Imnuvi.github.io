How you look at the world depends on the model you have of the world. And how you look at it determines how you act and what you do, and subsequently you become part of the world for others. ( You sow what you reap is an abstraction of this concept and how you react to these mental models. )

This need for a mental model arises from the fact that there are just too many variables in real life that you can track. In fact there are an infinite number of variables that you don't really have any control over. There is a sphere of influence surrounding each of us where we can exert some form and level of control. But usually this sphere is small and the total number of variables is simply infinite. This sphere grows with acquisition of wealth (Wealth here is used here vaguely to cover any form of transactional amount)

The mirror of control is observations. In classic control theory observability and controllability are complementary to each other. In our case, there are a lot more observable variables than control variables. But even these don't give the full picture of what is happening. (For example you can never know the true intention of someone's actions. You are going to have to take their word for it, with a certain level of certainty). Which means constructing the true reality behind your observations requires a model to put together all the information you observe.

When we grow up, our model is very vague and binary. Everything that happens has a straightforward cause and effect. But as we grow up, increasingly everything becomes Bayesian. This means you don't have fixed reactions to the same observations you make. The model becomes a probability of an event happening given all the prior information we know about the system at hand. The peanuts example of Charlie Brown kicking the football is the perfect example of this. Every time Lucy pulls the ball, Charlie learns something new about what Lucy thinks. But it's always the case that Lucy comes up with a new observation that Charlie has not seen and tricks him into falling on his back.

If we implement this to our everyday life, you can come up with a certainty value for each event that happens. Based on how many times the bus has been late before, you can determine with certain confidence if the bus will be late on that day. This applies equally to everything including how a person would react. And each interaction provides a new data point based on which you update your mental model.

I'm not saying this is how everything is, but knowingly or unknowingly we all do this inherently to certain extent.
